## 2. The S-election termination statement (attached to the 2025 Form 1120)

Signed by "Jeremiah Tallerine, President" on **October 10, 2026**, under penalties of perjury. It states that the S election "was automatically terminated effective January 1, 2025" because "on January 1, 2025, the corporation added an ineligible shareholder (a C corporation)," naming Comatri Inc. with **"Date of Transfer/Acquisition: January 1, 2025 … 1,000 shares which is a 9.34579 ownership percentage."**

The company's own records give different dates:

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| First Comatri investment agreement | Signed Jul 21–22, 2024; $15,000 for 3%, "effective July 22, 2024"; $15,010 received Jul 22, 2024 |
| Second Comatri investment agreement | Signed Nov 26, 2024; 1,000 shares of 10,700; $35,000 received Dec 2, 2024 |
| **The company's 2024 Form 1120-S** | Shares outstanding **10,000 at the start of 2024 and 10,700 at the end**; "6 shareholders during any part of the tax year" |
| **Hugh's 2024 K-1** | 1,000 shares all year; allocation percentage **9.93744%**, which is 1,000 ÷ ~10,063 weighted-average shares — i.e., the 700 new shares were treated as issued around the end of November 2024 |

So the 2024 return, filed in May 2025, already reports Comatri's shares as issued in 2024. The termination statement filed seventeen months later moves the acquisition to the first day of 2025, which is the first day on which a termination would leave the 2024 S-corporation return and the 2024 K-1s undisturbed. Whether that date was the CPA's simplification or a decision is a question for the meeting, not a conclusion. The consequences a specialist should be asked about: if the terminating event was July 22, 2024, the 2024 year splits into an S short year and a C short year (IRC §1362(e)), the 2024 1120-S and K-1s would need amending, and the normal cure is a request for inadvertent-termination relief under §1362(f), which the statement does not mention. The 2024 pass-through to Hugh was small (ordinary loss $6,744, rental income $1,788), so the dollar exposure for the Nguyens is modest; the record issue is the date.

Timing worth noting without characterizing it: Mark's text to Hugh on August 25, 2026 said the company was an S corporation; the statement converting it to a C corporation was signed October 10, 2026, and the return on October 31, 2026 — after the August 24 members' meeting. (A calendar-year Form 1120 on extension was due October 15, 2026; no tax is due, so the late-filing penalty is nil.)

## 3. The $69,033 is a loan on the tax return and a wage on Mark's draft

This is the single most useful reconciliation for tonight, because Hugh and Blake were handed both documents.

**Mark's draft, "Due from Jeremiah" $163,523 + "Due from James" $56,369 = $219,892**, is the QuickBooks figure: Due from Jer $75,441 + the 33500 owner-clearing debit $88,082 = $163,523. The **$74,364 difference Hugh spotted = $69,033 (AJE-547) + $5,331 (2025 draws)**, to the dollar. Mark keeps the $69,033 where QuickBooks put it, as a liability ("Accrued wages – Jeremiah" $176,914); the tax return puts it in the receivable from Jeremiah and carries no accrued-wage liability at all.

**Form 1120 line 13, salaries and wages, $62,919** is Gusto's 2025 wages per the ledger, $131,952.09, **less $69,033** (difference $69,033.09). The same $69,033 therefore (a) reduced the wage deduction below what was actually paid through payroll and (b) increased the loan the IRS is told Jeremiah owes the company. On the return's logic, Jeremiah was paid $69,033 more than his deductible salary and owes it back; on Mark's draft's logic, the company owes him $69,033 it never paid. Both documents were produced by the company's own advisors from the same ledger within the same month.

This also closes Hugh's question 5: $115,190 − $46,157 = $69,033 exactly. The July 2026 investor documents presented the 2025 loss without the accrual; the later draft includes it; the tax return excludes it again but on the other side of the balance sheet.

## 6. Where this leaves the "accrued wages" question

Three company-produced documents now take three positions on the same $69,033: the July 2026 investor documents leave it out of the loss; Mark's draft books it as a wage liability; the tax return treats it as money Jeremiah owes. Combined with the rebuilt cash analysis (only $39,992 of the $320,713 paid to the founder since 2023 was ever charged to accrued wages), the members can reasonably ask that the accrued-wage liability be withdrawn from every debt schedule and conversion proposal until the company states, in writing and consistently, which treatment it stands behind — and that the IRS be told the same thing the members are told.
