So the 2024 return, prepared in May 2025, records ownership changes on the two Comatri agreement dates and 10,700 shares outstanding from November 26, 2024 — while representing that every 2024 shareholder was an individual or domestic trust. The termination statement filed seventeen months later says Comatri acquired its shares on the first day of 2025, which is the first day on which a termination would leave the 2024 S-corporation return and the 2024 K-1s undisturbed. **The open factual question is in whose name the 2024 return placed those shares.** Only Hugh's and Christy's K-1s were provided; the other four would answer it. If a 2024 K-1 was issued to Comatri, the 2024 S return is inconsistent on its face; if one was issued to Blake Benthall personally, it names a person who was never the member (and the phrase "Date of Transfer/Acquisition" in the statement would imply a transfer that no document records). Whether that date was the CPA's simplification or a decision is a question for the meeting, not a conclusion. The consequences a specialist should be asked about: if the terminating event was July 22, 2024, the 2024 year splits into an S short year and a C short year (IRC §1362(e)), the 2024 1120-S and K-1s would need amending, and the normal cure is a request for inadvertent-termination relief under §1362(f), which the statement does not mention. The 2024 pass-through to Hugh was small (ordinary loss $6,744, rental income $1,788), so the dollar exposure for the Nguyens is modest; the record issue is the date.

Timing worth noting without characterizing it: Mark's text to Hugh on August 25, 2026 said the company was an S corporation; the statement converting it to a C corporation was signed October 10, 2026, and the return on October 31, 2026 — after the August 24 members' meeting. (A calendar-year Form 1120 on extension was due October 15, 2026; no tax is due, so the late-filing penalty is nil.)

**Form 1120 line 13, salaries and wages, $62,919** is Gusto's 2025 wages per the ledger, $131,952.09, **less $69,033** (difference $69,033.09). How line 13 was derived is a question for the CPA; the arithmetic is that an amount equal to AJE-547 is missing from the wage deduction and an amount equal to AJE-547 is inside the loan the IRS is told Jeremiah owes. On the return's presentation, Jeremiah owes the company that $69,033; on Mark's draft, the company owes it to him. Both documents were produced by the company's own advisors from the same ledger within the same month.

This also closes Hugh's question 5: $115,190 − $46,157 = $69,033 exactly. The July 2026 investor documents presented the 2025 loss without the accrual; the later draft includes it; the tax return excludes it again but on the other side of the balance sheet.

## 6. Where this leaves the "accrued wages" question

Company-produced documents now take different positions on the same $69,033: the July 2026 investor documents leave it out of the loss; Mark's draft books it as a wage liability; the tax return's balance sheet places it in what Jeremiah owes. And the three returns treat the accruals three different ways (§1a). Combined with the rebuilt cash analysis (only $39,992 of the $320,713 paid to the founder since 2023 was ever charged to accrued wages), the members can reasonably ask that the accrued-wage liability be withdrawn from every debt schedule and conversion proposal until the company states, in writing and consistently, which treatment it stands behind — and that the IRS be told the same thing the members are told.
