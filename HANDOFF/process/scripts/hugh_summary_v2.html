Six questions, in the order I would ask them

Each has the question, the background behind it, and what to ask for.

1
How much cash is there today, and what must be paid in the next 13 weeks?

Background

Mark's draft shows $1,963 in the bank on September 30 against $204,880 of unpaid bills, and $389,877 of obligations due within a year. Unpaid bills were $176,152 in the last ledger the members saw, on July 22, so they rose by about $28,700 in ten weeks.

A total does not show which bills are urgent. Some of the balance is older debt to the former co-packer, Endorphin Farms, and some is fees owed to Mark himself.

Ask for: the list of unpaid bills by vendor with due dates, and a week-by-week view of what will be paid through mid-February.

2
Who runs Bravado day to day, how many hours does Jer work on it, and for how long does he plan to continue?

Background

The July investor document says Jer "took multiple outside jobs to put more money into Bravado and to make skipping my own paychecks possible." It also planned for his pay to move to "$5K/month" from September.

During the same years the books recorded a salary for him that was not paid, as a debt the company owes him. The rate was $3,125 per half-month, or $75,000 a year: $68,750 for 2023, $71,875 for 2024 and $69,033 for 2025. The 2025 amount was recorded in a single entry made on February 26, 2026. The operating agreement says a manager's pay should be "commensurate with the value of such services."

So the hours he worked bear directly on whether the company owes him that salary, which now totals $176,914.

Ask: how many hours a week he works on Bravado now and worked in 2024 and 2025, who covers when he is away, whether the $5,000 a month has been paid since September, and who would take over the bank accounts, customers and books if he steps back.

3
How much does Jer owe the company, and when will it be repaid?

Background

Over several years the company sent money to Jer's personal accounts, or paid card accounts in his name, beyond what was recorded as salary or as documented business expenses. The accountants recorded the excess as money he owes back. It is now the largest asset on the balance sheet. On Mark's September figures, the amounts owed by Jer and by the former partner James Nelson are 80% of everything the company owns.

The company's own documents give four different figures for what Jer owes, shown in chart 2. The first two are for the same date and differ by $74,364. Almost all of that is one item of $69,033: QuickBooks and Mark's draft count it as salary the company owes Jer, while Finaloop and the tax return count the same amount as money Jer owes the company. The balance also includes a $12,135 check for a company truck sold in 2024, which the books record as received by Jer personally.

The tax return reports $294,256 owed by all shareholders: $237,887 by Jer and $56,369 by James Nelson. It shows no salary owed to Jer. Finaloop figures are from its ledger.

4
What has the company paid to Jer or on his behalf this year, and where are the receipts?

Background

Payroll stopped in March 2026, and no unpaid salary has been recorded for Jer this year. From January 1 to July 22 the ledger shows about $49,200 going to him or to card accounts in his name: $24,329 of card payments, $23,689 in 36 transfers to his personal checking account, $924 of personal charges and a $250 lease-application check.

Finaloop recorded about $18,500 of this as owner distributions and $27,640 as a business expense called "Reimbursements to owners." Mark's draft carries the same item as "Unallocated reimbursements," $31,312 for nine months. A reimbursement normally has receipts behind it. In earlier years the bookkeepers recorded itemized entries when Jer submitted business expenses, and he did pay real business costs personally. In 2025, $22,112 of the $118,128 paid to him was itemized that way. In 2026, nothing is.

Chart 3 shows each year's payments by how the bookkeepers treated them. Since 2023, $39,992 was treated as payment of his unpaid salary.

5
Which set of books is right, and what is being redone?

Background

The outside accounting firm's work in QuickBooks ended in March 2026, and the company moved to Finaloop. Finaloop's starting balances did not match where QuickBooks ended. The largest difference is the one in question 3: what Jer owes started $74,364 higher in Finaloop.

Mark's draft uses QuickBooks for December 2025 and Finaloop-based figures for September 2026. Some changes between his two columns therefore come from switching ledgers, not from anything that happened. For example, accumulated losses improve by $71,343 with no transaction to explain it.

On April 7, 2026 two Finaloop entries of $6,033.40 each reduced what Jer owes. One has no matching entry on the other side, which leaves the ledger out of balance by $5,873. Jer has told members the books are being redone, and the members have not seen the result.

Ask: is Mark's September column the redone version? Who gave Finaloop its starting balances, and who has reviewed the books since March? Is the $69,033 owed to Jer or by Jer? Ask that QuickBooks and the current Finaloop ledger be kept unchanged while anything is redone, so the before and after can be compared.

6
Why does the tax filing date Comatri's investment January 1, 2025, and does your 2024 K-1 still stand?

Background

Bravado has filed its taxes as an S corporation. In that form, profits and losses pass through to the owners on a K-1, and every owner must be an individual. Blake's investment was made through his company, Comatri, Inc. Mark's August text to you says this "is not allowed under S corp rules" and that he "had thought this adjustment was made" to make the investment personal to Blake. No such change was agreed.

The 2025 return was filed as a regular corporation instead. Attached to it is a statement, signed by Jer on October 10, 2026, saying Comatri acquired its shares on January 1, 2025. Comatri's agreements took effect on July 22 and November 26, 2024, and its money arrived on July 22 and December 2, 2024. The 2024 return already counts that $50,010 as owner capital at December 31, 2024 and reports six shareholders. The worksheet in your own K-1 package lists ownership changes on July 22 and November 26, 2024.

This matters to you because your 2024 K-1 passed a $6,744 loss and $1,788 of rental income to your personal return. If the S status ended in 2024 and not on January 1, 2025, the 2024 return and K-1s may need to be redone. That is a question for a tax adviser.

Separately, the returns show much smaller losses than the books, as chart 4 shows. The 2023 and 2024 returns use the cash method, which leaves out unpaid bills and unpaid salary. At the end of 2025 the return's balance sheet omits $353,084 of debts that are in QuickBooks.
