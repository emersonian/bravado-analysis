### 1. The pending netting, 2026 salary and write-offs
- **Facts.** Mark Clark's Nov 19 email says the manager's balance and accrued wages "should be netted," that 2026 compensation "will need to be added," and that the former partner's $56,369 "likely needs to be written off." At the earlier $75,000 rate (an assumption; no rate is stated) the result is about $25,200 owed to the manager. No agreement, rate-setting or member approval is in the records. The 8-page July version sent privately to the client states the plan in the manager's words: "My back pay ... absorbs the $75K I have drawn; the ~$101K net I will likely forgive or collect over years."
- **Frameworks.** Self-interested transaction by a fiduciary: presumed unfair, burden on the fiduciary (*Holloway*; *Greathouse*); safe harbour for disclosed, disinterested approval or fairness (TBOC 101.255). Operating Agreement 5.1 sets a standard, no rate or approver; 4.7 shields only good-faith acts and only liability "to the Members." For creditors: an obligation incurred, and an insider's old claim satisfied, while liabilities exceed assets. For tax: netting is a wage payment, with roughly $66,000–$81,000 of deposits due against $1,963 of cash (illustrative).
- **Who holds it.** The company (a member may sue derivatively without demand in a closely held LLC, TBOC 101.463); creditors separately.
- **Strength.** Strong. It is in writing before it is done.
- **Best answer to it.** 5.1 entitles him to reasonable pay; the rate matches his earlier payroll rate; 4.3(f) lets managers compromise claims; netting is ordinary bookkeeping.
- **Objection now on record.** After the reply-all of Nov 19, the client sent the members: "For the record, Comatri does not agree to any compensation being accrued for Jer for 2026, or to any netting of accrued wages against the balance he owes the company, until the members have seen the basis for the rate and the periods, the payroll tax treatment, and have agreed to it in writing."

  - That description sits against four company documents: the books record the same cash as owed by the manager; the 2025 Form 1120 reports $294,256 as loans to shareholders; the 2023 and 2024 returns show officer compensation of $10,039 and $3,694; the July 2026 update says he "skipped my own paychecks."
  - In the same thread, $31,312 of 2026 payments is described as "reimbursement to Jer for company expenses he paid personally." A payment cannot be both a reimbursement of a business cost and a draw.
  - His two emails describe two different adjustments. Reclassifying what was actually drawn leaves the manager owing roughly $31,000 on the September figures ($208,100 less $177,063). Accruing nine months of 2026 at $75,000 a year first, then netting, produces about $25,200 owed to him. The difference is about $56,000, and which is intended is not stated.
  - If draws were compensation when taken, payroll tax was due in the years taken.

No disinterested officer or manager existed who could have approved his pay; only the members could, and no request to them is in the records. The same fact cuts the other way: in a business this small an owner-manager setting a modest salary is ordinary, and some pay for years he ran the company is likely to be allowed. The argument is therefore about amount and process, year by year:

### 2. Comatri's tax position and what it must not sign
- **Facts.** A statement attached to the 2025 Form 1120, signed by the manager on 10/10/2026 with its own penalties-of-perjury declaration, dates Comatri's acquisition January 1, 2025. Both agreements, both deposits, the 2024 return's paid-in capital and share count, and the 2024 K-1 worksheet place it in 2024.
- **Frameworks.** Termination takes effect on the date eligibility ends and is not elective (IRC 1362(d)(2)). A 2024 date splits 2024 into an S and a C short year and moves about 44% of each individual member's 2024 K-1 items back to the company. Relief for an inadvertent termination (1362(f)) needs Comatri's signature and its exit as holder.
- **Exposure.** Comatri appears to owe no tax of its own on any reading. Its exposure is what it signs. The statement also helps Comatri: it tells the IRS that Comatri Inc., not an individual, holds 1,000 shares.
- **New point.** The Operating Agreement liquidates by capital account while members paid different amounts per share, which may be a second class of stock. If so the election had a defect unrelated to Comatri. A tax practitioner must confirm.
- **Do not sign without independent tax advice:** anything changing the holder or date; any IRS-facing shareholder consent or statement; a W-9 for the interest in the client's own name; any acknowledgment that Comatri caused the termination; any release bundled with a "fix."
- **Conflict to note.** The Nguyens hold 2024 K-1s that a 2024 termination date would disturb. Their interest on the date differs from Comatri's.

### 6. The client's April 2026 advance (personal exposure)
- **Facts.** 7,500 USDC for "a 8500 return in a month," requested by text in the first person, to a wallet the manager supplied. The company's own debt schedule lists "Blake Benthall $7,500."
- **Framework.** About 160% a year against an 18% commercial ceiling. A lender who "contracts for or receives" excess interest on a commercial transaction is liable for three times the excess, about $2,670, plus the borrower's fees (Fin. Code 305.001(a-1), 305.005). No principal forfeiture or criminal provision on the commercial reading.
- **Cure.** A written correction within 60 days of actual discovery, before the borrower gives notice (305.103).
- **Until then.** No letter, request or pleading should state $8,500 as owed. The borrower's identity is open; a notice would sensibly go to both the company and the manager.
- **The claim side.** The April 29, 2026 text ("I have a lock on the next 300k from SBA") is the one 2026 statement on which someone acted and lost money: the client lent on it, and no such advance appears in any record. It is the client's personal claim, weak to moderate as an inducement theory because "a lock" is partly prediction.

### 7. Control and remedies: what is and is not available
- **Not available:** removal (needs a majority he holds); a common-law oppression buyout (*Ritchie v. Rupe*). A receiver is a last resort and the company has no cash to fund one. The manager can vote dissolution alone (Operating Agreement 1.4(a)).
- **Available to a 9% member alone:** records demand; preservation notice and written objections; refusing consents (amendment, new interests, new members, the tax fix); a derivative or declaratory claim.
- **Negotiated:** standstill; a note for the validated balance secured by his interest; pay fixed in advance by disinterested members; a second signer and monthly exports. A debt-for-interest redemption looks barred while liabilities exceed assets.
- **Joint representation with the Nguyens** has real conflicts: lender against equity, conversion price, the tax date, and no confidentiality between joint clients.
- **Leverage if the co-investors are not aligned.** The note, the refusal to defer it and the creditor-only claims on 2026 payments belong to the Nguyens. What Comatri and the client hold alone:
  1. Comatri's signature: the usual S-corporation fix, any new member, new interests or amendment appear to need it.
  2. A claim that may reach the manager personally: the Texas Securities Act claim on the $50,010 (about $56,000 with interest), plus the client's loan.
  3. The company's claims against the manager, brought derivatively: his balance, the 2026 distributions, the unapproved salary.
  4. Records access with fee-shifting on refusal of a proper demand.
  5. A credible offer for the assets (section 7).
  Alone, that is enough to block and to sue, not to force a change of manager.
- **By goal.**

| Goal | Realistic for Comatri alone? | Why |

| Buy the brand and operating assets | Possible | Needs the manager and the SBA to agree, or a creditor to force a sale |

- **Not to be done.** A resignation or settlement must never be tied to reporting anyone to a tax or other authority, or to publicizing findings.

## 7. Possible purchase of the assets (added November 20)

The client is considering buying the brand and operating assets and leaving the entity behind. Counsel should be told before any records demand goes out.

- **Routes.** A negotiated purchase from the company with the secured lender's release; a purchase from the lender or a bankruptcy trustee; or a court-supervised sale of the operating business with the client as opening ("stalking horse") bidder. Only the company or its creditors can start a bankruptcy; a member cannot.
- **Negotiated versus forced.** A negotiated sale hands over a running business with accounts, listings, recipes and introductions, at a higher price, and needs the manager's signature. A forced sale delivers assets as they sit, with little information and no help, at a lower price, usually under a court order clearing old debts.
- **Rough value (an estimate from the company's own figures, not an appraisal).** Brand, recipes, listings and customer accounts: about $50,000 to $125,000 negotiated, $25,000 to $60,000 forced. Inventory: perhaps $25,000 to $45,000 against $59,829 in the draft, which its preparer says is wrong. Basis: sales of about $325,000 a year and falling ($805,745 in 2024; $464,997 in 2025; $243,334 for nine months of 2026); no operating profit; marketing spend of only about $8,000 in seven months of 2026, which suggests repeat and organic sales; channels spread across wholesale 30%, the Shopify store 24%, Amazon 21% and Faire 18%.
- **Open points.** Who owns the trademark and recipes (the company or the manager personally); the SBA's lien, on which about $349,000 is owed and which would be paid first; the true margin (32% in 2025, 53% shown for 2026 on an inventory figure said to be wrong); who holds the wholesale accounts; and how fast sales are still falling.
- **Client's view, not a document:** the manager "barely works" on the business, so the brand would transfer without him.
